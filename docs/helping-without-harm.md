# Helping without harm: enabling, perpetuating, and true help

People often ask how to help someone who is homeless. The answer is not always money, a tent, or a bed.
Sometimes the most important help is a respectful conversation, a bottle of water, a service referral,
help replacing a document, or simply treating someone as a person whose “no” matters.

## Enabling is not the same as helping

“Enabling” is often used as a judgment about another person. Use the word carefully. Support becomes
unsafe or counterproductive when it repeatedly removes accountability for serious harm, funds exploitation,
creates dependency without choice, or requires the helper to ignore danger. It can also mean rescuing in a
way that takes away the other person’s voice and control.

That does not mean every gift, ride, meal, or place to sleep is enabling. Do not use the label to shame
someone for surviving, using a service, or accepting help. Ask what the person wants, what is safe, and
whether the arrangement can continue without coercion.

## Perpetuating harm

Help can unintentionally perpetuate the conditions it claims to address when it:

- demands a traumatic story or personal data before offering basic respect;
- gives items without asking what is useful, safe, accessible, or culturally appropriate;
- treats a person as a project, publicity opportunity, or lesson for donors;
- moves people along without a safe destination, separates them from belongings, or calls enforcement
  when a non-emergency conversation or outreach option would be safer;
- makes promises that cannot be kept, then blames the person for trusting them; or
- publishes a name, face, camp, health detail, or recovery credential “to get them help.”

Good intentions do not erase these effects. The repair is to listen, ask permission, correct the record,
and change the practice.

## What true help often looks like

True help preserves agency and safety. It may be:

- saying hello and using the person’s chosen name without demanding their history;
- asking, “Would information, an item, or space be helpful?” and accepting “no”;
- sharing 2-1-1, a day center, street medicine, food, hygiene, document, or legal-aid information;
- helping make a call, read a form, replace an ID, charge a phone, or reach an appointment—if invited;
- offering a specific item or amount with no public performance and no strings attached;
- respecting quiet, privacy, boundaries, pets, belongings, pronouns, language, and communication needs;
- supporting a person’s own plan instead of taking control; and
- keeping your own limits clear: you may leave, say no, ask for backup, and avoid unsafe situations.

Respect is not a consolation prize until a bed appears. Dignity, truthful information, consent, and a
safe relationship are real forms of help. They can make it possible for someone to ask again tomorrow.

## A simple check before acting

1. **Ask:** What would be useful to you right now?
2. **Offer choices:** Give one or two realistic options, not a command.
3. **Protect privacy:** Do not photograph, post, or collect details without consent.
4. **Keep promises small and honest:** Say what you can and cannot do.
5. **Stay safe:** Do not promise secrecy in an emergency or put yourself at risk.
6. **Follow up only with permission:** A person is not obligated to report back or show gratitude.

For immediate danger use emergency services. For current San Diego referrals, start with 2-1-1. On Record
is not a live shelter-placement system and should never be used to locate a person.
